    Two Courts, Opposite Verdicts: The KPSS Test

    Every test we have met so far works like a strict court: the series is presumed to be a random walk, and it is up to the data to prove stationarity beyond reasonable doubt. When the evidence is thin, which it often is, the verdict is "not proven", and lazy researchers write "the series has a unit root".

    In 1992 four econometricians asked a cheeky question. What if we built a court with the opposite presumption? Their paper's subtitle says it all: "How sure are we that economic time series have a unit root?"

    Four names you will mispronounce

    The paper is Kwiatkowski, Phillips, Schmidt and Shin (1992), "Testing the null hypothesis of stationarity against the alternative of a unit root", Journal of Econometrics, volume 54. Everyone calls the test KPSS, partly out of laziness and partly because nobody is sure how to say Kwiatkowski (roughly kfyat-KOF-skee, if you want to impress a Polish friend).

    The P is Peter Phillips again, from Chapter 4. By now you may suspect that every unit root test has a Phillips in it somewhere. You would not be far wrong. The S is Peter Schmidt of Michigan State University, and the other S is Yongcheol Shin.

    The idea: a series made of two parts

    KPSS imagine that the series is the sum of a deterministic part, a random walk, and a stationary error:

    \[ y_t = \xi\, t + r_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad r_t = r_{t-1} + u_t, \quad \operatorname{Var}(u_t) = \sigma_u^2 \]

    The random walk \(r_t\) is the balloon hiding inside the series. Its size is controlled by \(\sigma_u^2\):

    • If \(\sigma_u^2 = 0\), the random walk never moves. \(r_t\) is just a constant, and \(y_t\) is stationary (around a trend if \(\xi \neq 0\)).
    • If \(\sigma_u^2 > 0\), the balloon is inside, and \(y_t\) has a unit root.

    So the KPSS null hypothesis is

    \[ H_0: \sigma_u^2 = 0 \ (\text{stationary}) \qquad H_1: \sigma_u^2 > 0 \ (\text{unit root}) \]

    The statistic: watch the running total

    Here is the clever part. Regress \(y_t\) on a constant (or a constant and trend) and keep the residuals \(e_t\). Then add them up as you go, a running total:

    \[ S_t = e_1 + e_2 + \dots + e_t \]

    If the series is stationary, the residuals bounce above and below zero and keep cancelling each other. The running total stays small. If there is a random walk inside, the residuals come in long runs of the same sign (the balloon drifts up for a while, then down), and the running total swings far from zero.

    The KPSS statistic measures how big the running total gets:

    \[ \eta = \frac{1}{T^2}\, \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} S_t^2}{\hat f_0} \]

    where \(\hat f_0\) is the long-run variance of the residuals, estimated with the same Newey-West method as in the Phillips-Perron chapter. Large values of \(\eta\) reject stationarity. It is a Lagrange multiplier (LM) test, which is why EViews labels it "LM-Stat."

    The critical values are fixed, and short enough to remember:

    Model 10% 5% 1%
    Constant (level stationary) 0.347 0.463 0.739
    Constant + trend (trend stationary) 0.119 0.146 0.216

    Careful: KPSS rejects for large values. ADF rejects for large negative values. Students mix these up in exams every single year.

    Putting the two courts together

    Running both tests is called confirmatory analysis. There are four possible outcomes:

    KPSS does not reject (looks stationary) KPSS rejects (looks like a unit root)
    ADF rejects (looks stationary) Stationary. Both agree. Conflict. Check for breaks, outliers or long memory.
    ADF does not reject (looks like a unit root) The data can't decide. Often too short or too persistent. Unit root. Both agree.

    The kite sits in the top-left box. The balloon and GDP sit in the bottom-right box. Those are the comfortable cases.

    The uncomfortable case is bottom-left. Here is a series from the sheet P14_mystery: 100 observations, no trend.

    • ADF with a constant: statistic −2.33, p = 0.16. Cannot reject a unit root.
    • KPSS with a constant: η = 0.182. Cannot reject stationarity.

    Neither court is willing to convict. This happens a lot with short, persistent series. The data simply don't contain enough information. Is MYSTERY a kite on a very long string, or a balloon? We will crack it in Chapter 6, with sharper tests.

    Two cautions about KPSS

    1. Bandwidth matters. KPSS depends on the long-run variance, which depends on the bandwidth. For a stationary but very persistent series, KPSS tends to reject too often. If KPSS is a close call, try a different bandwidth and see if the verdict survives.
    2. It is not a magic fix. Swapping the null hypothesis does not create information. It only changes who bears the burden of proof. That is exactly why using both tests is more honest than using either alone.

    The lesson

    Whenever someone tells you "the series has a unit root because the ADF test could not reject", ask them what KPSS said. A failure to reject is not proof. Two courts with opposite presumptions reaching the same verdict is the closest thing to proof that time series econometrics offers.

    Something to think about

    You run ADF and KPSS on India's monthly inflation since 2000 and land in the top-right box: ADF says stationary, KPSS says unit root. The RBI adopted a formal inflation target in 2016. Could that explain the conflict? Which test do you think is being fooled, and why? Answer in the comments; Chapter 7 will help.
