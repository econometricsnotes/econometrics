    The Kite and the Balloon: What on Earth Is a Unit Root?

    Two children are standing in a field on a windy afternoon. One is flying a kite. The other is holding a balloon. A gust of wind arrives and pushes both of them sideways. A minute later the kite is back roughly where it was, bobbing about at the end of its string. The balloon slipped out of a small hand and is now drifting towards the next village. Nobody is going to see it again.

    That, in one picture, is the difference between a stationary time series and one with a unit root. This post starts a new series on this blog: eight chapters on how econometricians tell the kite from the balloon.

    A drunk man, a mosquito and a letter to Nature

    The idea behind this whole series was born in 1905, in a letter to the journal Nature. Karl Pearson, one of the founders of modern statistics, asked readers for help with what he called "the problem of the random walk". A man starts at a lamp post, takes a step of fixed length in a random direction, then another, then another. After n steps, where is he likely to be?

    The funny part is where the question came from. Pearson had been helping Sir Ronald Ross, who won the Nobel Prize in Medicine for showing that mosquitoes carry malaria. Ross wanted to know how mosquitoes spread out from a breeding pond. Pearson told Ross that mathematicians would ignore a "mosquito problem", so he dressed it up as a walking man instead. The library of the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine still has the correspondence.

    Lord Rayleigh answered within a week. Pearson then summed up the result with a line every econometrics student should know. In open country, he wrote, the most probable place to find a drunken man who can still stand is somewhere near his starting point.

    Hold on to that sentence. It is true, and it is also a trap. The drunk's most likely position is near the lamp post, but the range of places he could be grows every minute. That growing range is the heart of a unit root.

    The model: one number decides everything

    Let the height of our kite or balloon be \(y_t\) in minute \(t\). The simplest model of a series that depends on its own past is the first-order autoregression, AR(1):

    \[ y_t = \rho \, y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \]

    Here \(\varepsilon_t\) is the gust of wind in minute \(t\): random, mean zero, variance \(\sigma^2\), independent from one minute to the next. Everything depends on the single number \(\rho\) (rho).

    • If \(|\rho| < 1\), the series is stationary: the kite.
    • If \(\rho = 1\), the series is a random walk: the balloon.

    What happens to a gust of wind? A short derivation

    Substitute the equation into itself again and again:

    \[ y_t = \rho y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t = \rho(\rho y_{t-2} + \varepsilon_{t-1}) + \varepsilon_t = \dots \]
    \[ y_t = \rho^t y_0 + \varepsilon_t + \rho\,\varepsilon_{t-1} + \rho^2 \varepsilon_{t-2} + \dots + \rho^{t-1}\varepsilon_1 \]

    Read the right-hand side slowly. A gust that hit \(j\) minutes ago is still in today's height, multiplied by \(\rho^j\).

    • With \(\rho = 0.6\), a gust from 5 minutes ago has weight \(0.6^5 = 0.08\). Almost forgotten.
    • With \(\rho = 1\), the weight is \(1^j = 1\) for ever. Nothing is ever forgotten.

    That is why a series with \(\rho = 1\) is said to have permanent shocks or infinite memory.

    A useful way to feel \(\rho\) is the half-life: how long until half of a shock has gone. Solve \(\rho^h = 0.5\):

    \[ h = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln \rho} \]
    \(\rho\) Half-life of a shock Feels like
    0.60 1.4 periods a kite on a short string
    0.90 6.6 periods a kite on a long string
    0.99 69 periods a kite on a very, very long string
    1.00 never a balloon

    The variance: fixed band versus widening trumpet

    Derivation: the variance of a stationary AR(1) (click to open)

    If the series is stationary, its variance is the same in every period. Call it \(\gamma_0\). Take the variance of both sides of \(y_t = \rho y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\). Because \(\varepsilon_t\) is independent of \(y_{t-1}\):

    \[ \gamma_0 = \rho^2 \gamma_0 + \sigma^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \gamma_0 = \frac{\sigma^2}{1-\rho^2} \]

    This only makes sense if \(1 - \rho^2 > 0\), that is \(|\rho| < 1\). As \(\rho \to 1\) the variance explodes. The formula is telling us that a random walk has no fixed variance at all.

    Derivation: the variance of a random walk (click to open)

    With \(\rho = 1\) and starting value \(y_0\):

    \[ y_t = y_0 + \varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 + \dots + \varepsilon_t \]

    The shocks are independent, so their variances add up:

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(y_t \mid y_0) = t\,\sigma^2 \]

    The variance grows in a straight line with time. The standard deviation grows like \(\sqrt{t}\).

    So a stationary series lives inside a band of fixed width. A random walk lives inside a trumpet that keeps opening. Here are 150 simulated kites and 150 simulated balloons, all hit by exactly the same gusts:

    This also settles Pearson's drunk. The centre of the trumpet is the lamp post, so the most likely single spot is near home. But the trumpet keeps widening. After 100 steps he is typically 10 steps away; after 10,000 steps, typically 100 steps away.

    Why is it called a "unit root"?

    Write the lag operator \(L\), which shifts a series back one period: \(L y_t = y_{t-1}\). The AR(1) model becomes

    \[ (1 - \rho L)\, y_t = \varepsilon_t \]

    Treat \(1 - \rho z = 0\) as an ordinary equation in \(z\). Its root is \(z = 1/\rho\). When \(\rho = 1\), the root is exactly 1: a unit root. That's all the name means. The fancier statement "all roots lie outside the unit circle" just says \(|1/\rho| > 1\), which is \(|\rho| < 1\): the kite.

    Integrated series and differencing

    If \(y_t\) is a random walk, its first difference is pure noise:

    \[ \Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1} = \varepsilon_t \]

    Econometricians say \(y_t\) is integrated of order one, written I(1): difference it once and it becomes stationary, I(0). The balloon's height wanders, but its change in height each minute is just the gust.

    The data

    The sheet P9_kite_balloon has 200 minutes of two simulated series:

    • KITE: an AR(1) with \(\rho = 0.6\) around a mean of 50 metres.
    • BALLOON: a random walk starting at 50 metres.

    Both are driven by gusts with the same standard deviation of 3 metres.

    Look at the numbers. In the first 100 minutes the kite averages 50.2 m, and in the last 100 minutes 51.1 m. The balloon averages 27.4 m in the first half and 71.2 m in the second. Its spread more than doubles too, from 8.4 m to 17.6 m. The kite has a home. The balloon does not.

    For the balloon, the autocorrelations start at 0.99 and are still 0.85 at lag 12. They shrink so slowly that you can almost hear them yawn. The partial autocorrelation has one giant spike at lag 1 and almost nothing else. That pattern, a slowly dying AC with one big PAC spike near 1, is the classic fingerprint of a unit root.

    Why does the random walk's autocorrelation stay so high?

    For a random walk started at zero, \(y_t\) and \(y_{t-k}\) share all the shocks up to time \(t-k\). A little algebra gives

    \[ \operatorname{Corr}(y_t, y_{t-k}) = \sqrt{\frac{t-k}{t}} \]

    With \(t = 200\) and \(k = 12\), this is \(\sqrt{188/200} = 0.97\). The correlation is close to 1 for any lag that is small compared with the length of the sample.

    Why should anyone care?

    Because the balloon breaks ordinary regression. Remember Post 2 on this blog: two completely unrelated random walks, popcorn sales and goldfish sales, produced a t-statistic of 22. Clive Granger and Paul Newbold showed in 1974 how often this happens, and called such results "spurious regressions".

    Granger's story has a happy ending. David Hendry criticised the Granger–Newbold work, and Granger set out to prove Hendry wrong. In the process he discovered cointegration: the idea that two wandering series can wander together. Granger shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Economics for it, with Robert Engle. Hendry and Jennifer Castle tell the story in their tribute Clive W.J. Granger and Cointegration. Losing an argument rarely pays that well.

    So before you regress anything on anything over time, you need to know whether you are holding kites or balloons. The rest of this series is about how to find out:

    A little game

    Here are some real-world series. Kite or balloon? (Answers are argued about in journals, so there may be more than one right answer.)

    1. The rupee–dollar exchange rate.
    2. India's monthly inflation rate.
    3. The Sensex.
    4. The unemployment rate.
    5. The interest rate gap between a 10-year and a 1-year government bond.

    Most economists would say the exchange rate and the Sensex look like balloons. Inflation, unemployment and the spread are usually treated as kites, though some of them have very long strings. Louis Bachelier argued as early as 1900, in his Paris thesis on speculation, that stock prices move like a random walk. He was ignored for half a century.

    The lesson

    One number, \(\rho\), separates a world where shocks fade from a world where they last for ever. When \(\rho < 1\), history eventually stops mattering. When \(\rho = 1\), every gust of wind that ever blew is still in today's number.

    Something to think about

    Suppose a country's GDP is a balloon. A bad monsoon cuts GDP by 2% this year. What does the unit root say about GDP ten years from now? Now suppose GDP is a kite. What changes? Which story do you believe for India, and why? Leave your answer in the comments.
