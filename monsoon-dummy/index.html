    The Monsoon Dummy

    In Kerala, nobody needs an econometrician to tell them when umbrellas sell. The southwest monsoon arrives around the start of June and stays until September. Schools reopen in early June, so late May brings a rush of parents buying umbrellas, raincoats and school bags.

    About this post

    Concept: dummy variables, seasonality and the dummy variable trap · Data sheet: P6_monsoon · Simulated monthly data, 2015M01–2024M12, 120 observations

    Download the data (Excel) — sheet P6_monsoon · EViews program

    So why write a post about it? Because umbrellas give us the cleanest possible lesson on dummy variables: variables that are simply 1 or 0, "yes" or "no".

    The data

    A chain of shops records UMBRELLA sales (thousand units) each month. We also create:

    • TREND — 1, 2, 3, … to capture slow growth over the years
    • MONSOON — 1 in June, July, August and September; 0 otherwise
    • MAY — 1 in May (the school-reopening rush); 0 otherwise

    In EViews you can make the dummies in one line each: series monsoon = @month>=6 and @month<=9 and series may = @month=5.

    Model 1: ignore the season

    A miserable model. The R-squared is 0.02, and the trend is not even significant at 5%. The Durbin-Watson of 0.66 shouts that the residuals have a pattern. Of course they do: every June to September they are large and positive, and every other month they are negative. The season is hiding in the error term.

    Now read each line like a sentence:

    • C = 18.5: in a dry month (not May, not monsoon), at the start of the sample, the shops sell about 18,500 umbrellas.
    • MONSOON = 41.3: in a monsoon month, sales are about 41,300 higher than in a dry month.
    • MAY = 11.7: May sales are about 11,700 higher than an ordinary dry month.
    • TREND = 0.079: underneath the seasons, sales grow by about 79 umbrellas a month.

    See what happened to TREND. In Model 1 it was insignificant (t = 1.7). Now t = 7.6. The trend was always there. It was simply drowned out by the seasonal noise we had refused to model. Controlling for something obvious can make a subtle effect visible.

    The dummy variable trap

    An eager student thinks: "Why not have a dummy for the dry season too?" They create series dry = 1 - monsoon and type ls umbrella c trend monsoon dry may. EViews replies:

    Near singular matrix error. Regressors may be perfectly collinear.

    Here is why. For every month, MONSOON + DRY = 1. And the constant C is also a column of 1s. So C is exactly equal to MONSOON + DRY. OLS is being asked to share out the same information three ways, and there is no unique answer. This is perfect multicollinearity, and it has a famous name: the dummy variable trap.

    The rule: if a category has m groups, include m − 1 dummies with a constant. The group you leave out becomes the base, and every dummy coefficient is a difference from that base.

    Or drop the constant and keep all m dummies: ls umbrella trend monsoon dry may

    Same fit, same residuals, same R-squared. Only the labels changed. Now each dummy gives a level instead of a difference: 59.8 thousand in a monsoon month, 18.5 in a dry month. And 59.8 − 18.5 = 41.3, exactly the MONSOON coefficient from Model 2. (EViews drops the F-statistic here because there is no constant to test against.)

    The lesson

    A dummy variable is a way of letting the intercept move. It lets the data say, "in this group, everything starts from a different level." The choice of base group is yours; the story the model tells is the same.

    Something to think about

    The MAY dummy assumes the pre-monsoon rush is the same every year. Suppose the monsoon arrived on 22 May one year and on 8 June another. How would you change the model to let the data tell you whether an early monsoon steals sales from June or adds new ones?
