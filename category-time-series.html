Two Courts, Opposite Verdicts: The KPSS Test

When the evidence is thin, which it often is, the verdict is "not proven", and lazy researchers write "the series has a unit root".

In 1992 four econometricians asked a cheeky question. What if we built a court with the opposite presumption? Their paper's subtitle says it all: "How sure are we that economic time series have a unit root?"

Giving the Test a Memory: The Augmented Dickey-Fuller Test

The plain Dickey-Fuller test makes one heroic assumption: the shocks that push the series around are completely unpredictable, like coin tosses. Real economies are not like that. A good quarter for business tends to be followed by another good quarter. Monsoon effects spill over several months. If yesterday's shock tells you something about today's, the Dickey-Fuller test is reading the wrong table again.

The cure, invented by Said E. Said and David Dickey, is almost embarrassingly simple: give the regression a memory.

Residuals That Remember Yesterday: Autocorrelation

If today is hotter than usual, tomorrow probably will be too. Heat waves last for days; cold spells too. Moods carry over from one morning to the next. Prices that rose last month often keep rising this month. Time has memory.

When that memory sneaks into the errors of a regression, we have autocorrelation, also called serial correlation. It doesn't make the coefficients wrong, but it makes the regression far too confident about them. This chapter shows how to spot it, test it and fix it, with an electricity company that badly needs to know how much a hot month costs it.
