    The Two Phillipses: A Crocodile Hunter, a Water Computer, and the Phillips-Perron Test

    New Zealand is a small country with a population of around five million. It has produced two of the most famous econometricians in history, and both of them are called Phillips. Neither is related to the other. One built a working model of the British economy out of water tanks and pipes. The other gave us the mathematics of wandering time series, including the unit root test in this chapter.

    Let me introduce them, and then show you what the Phillips-Perron test does differently from the ADF.

    Phillips number one: Bill

    Alban William Housego Phillips, known as Bill, was born in 1914 at Te Rehunga, near Dannevirke, in New Zealand. He left school early and worked his way around Australia. Among his jobs: crocodile hunter and cinema manager.

    In the Second World War he served with the Royal Air Force and was captured when Java fell to the Japanese. He spent about three and a half years in a prisoner-of-war camp. There he learned Chinese from other prisoners, and he repaired and miniaturised a secret radio so the prisoners could hear the news. He also built a little water boiler for tea that ran off the camp's lighting system.

    After the war he studied at the London School of Economics. In 1949, over one summer, in his landlady's garage in Croydon, he built the MONIAC: a machine about two metres tall in which coloured water flows through tanks and pipes to show how income, spending, taxes and savings move around an economy. It cost about £400 to build. Somewhere between twelve and fourteen were made; one still works at the University of Cambridge, and others are on display at the Science Museum in London and the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Museum.

    In 1958 he published the paper that made his name immortal, on the relationship between unemployment and the rate of change of money wages in the UK. You know it as the Phillips curve. He died in Auckland in 1975.

    Phillips number two: Peter

    Peter C. B. Phillips studied economics and mathematics at the University of Auckland and completed a PhD in econometrics at the LSE in 1974, the same school where Bill had built his water computer. He moved to Yale in 1979 and has been there ever since; he is now Sterling Professor Emeritus. He founded the journal Econometric Theory, has supervised more than 100 PhD students, and his papers have been cited well over 100,000 times. Much of what we know today about regressions with random walks comes from him.

    In 1988 Peter Phillips and Pierre Perron published "Testing for a unit root in time series regression" in Biometrika. Perron will come back in Chapter 7 with one of the most famous papers in macroeconomics.

    So: one Phillips poured water through pipes to show how the economy flows. The other Phillips wrote the theory of how economic series wander. Both studied at the LSE. Both came from New Zealand. If you ever need a pub quiz question for econometricians, you are welcome.

    The problem, again

    Back in Chapter 3, the ADF test dealt with autocorrelated shocks by adding lagged differences to the regression. That is a parametric fix: we model the memory explicitly.

    Phillips and Perron had a different idea. Keep the simple Dickey-Fuller regression with no lags at all:

    \[ \Delta y_t = \alpha + \delta\, y_{t-1} + u_t \]

    Let \(u_t\) be autocorrelated, or have changing variance, or both. Then correct the t-statistic afterwards for the damage this does. That is a non-parametric fix: we don't model the memory, we just measure how much of it there is.

    Short-run variance and long-run variance

    The key idea is the difference between two variances of the residuals.

    • The short-run variance \(\gamma_0\): how big the shocks are in a single month.
    • The long-run variance \(f_0\): how big their accumulated effect is over many months.

    If shocks are independent, these two are the same. If a positive shock tends to be followed by more positive shocks, the shocks pile up, and the long-run variance is bigger than the short-run variance. Think of rain: one heavy shower (short-run) is different from a week of showers that keep coming (long-run).

    The long-run variance is estimated with a weighted sum of autocovariances, the Newey-West estimator with a Bartlett kernel:

    \[ \hat f_0 = \hat\gamma_0 + 2\sum_{j=1}^{\ell} \left(1 - \frac{j}{\ell + 1}\right)\hat\gamma_j \]

    where \(\hat\gamma_j\) is the autocovariance of the residuals at lag \(j\) and \(\ell\) is the bandwidth: how many lags we look at. EViews chooses \(\ell\) automatically.

    The Phillips-Perron statistic, as EViews computes it, is

    \[ \tilde t_\delta = t_\delta \left(\frac{\hat\gamma_0}{\hat f_0}\right)^{1/2} - \frac{T(\hat f_0 - \hat\gamma_0)\,\operatorname{se}(\hat\delta)}{2 \hat f_0^{1/2}\, s} \]

    where \(t_\delta\) and \(\operatorname{se}(\hat\delta)\) are the t-statistic and standard error from the simple regression, and \(s\) is its standard error of regression. Don't panic at the formula. Look at what it does: if \(\hat f_0 = \hat\gamma_0\), the correction vanishes and you get back the ordinary Dickey-Fuller t-statistic. The bigger the gap between long-run and short-run variance, the bigger the correction. The corrected statistic uses the same Dickey-Fuller critical values.

    The data

    The sheet P12_phillips_perron has 20 years of monthly data on SPREAD, the gap in percentage points between a short-term lending rate and the policy rate of an imaginary central bank. I built it as a stationary series (\(\rho = 0.85\)) whose shocks:

    • have a moving-average part: each month's shock echoes into the next month, and
    • arrive in bursts: a few calm years, then a few months of large shocks, like a market under stress.

    So the truth is: SPREAD is a kite, but with untidy, autocorrelated, heteroskedastic gusts.

    Let me walk through it.

    The test equation at the bottom is the plain Dickey-Fuller regression. Its t-statistic on SPREAD(-1) is −2.75. On its own, against a 5% critical value of −2.87, that would not reject the unit root. But the Durbin-Watson stat of 1.12 warns that the residuals are autocorrelated, so the plain DF test is not reliable here.

    The variances in the middle box tell the story. The residual variance is 0.053, but the HAC (long-run) variance is 0.077, about 44% larger. The shocks pile up.

    The adjusted t-statistic is −3.32, with p = 0.014. After the correction, we reject the unit root at the 5% level. SPREAD is stationary, which is the truth.

    For comparison, the ADF test with SIC lags (1 lag chosen) gives −4.68, p = 0.0001. Both fixes, the parametric and the non-parametric, reach the right answer here. The naive test, without any fix, would have got it wrong.

    ADF or PP?

    ADF Phillips-Perron
    How it handles memory Adds lagged differences Corrects the t-statistic afterwards
    Choice you must make Number of lags Bandwidth (EViews automates it)
    Changing variance (heteroskedasticity) Not designed for it Robust to it
    Weak spot Wrong lag length Large negative moving-average shocks (the test rejects far too often)

    That weak spot is serious. G. William Schwert showed in 1989 that when shocks have a strong negative moving-average component, Phillips-Perron tests can reject a true unit root most of the time. Many applied researchers therefore report ADF as the main test and PP as a robustness check. When the two agree, you can relax. When they disagree, look at the residual autocorrelations before believing either.

    The lesson

    There are two ways to deal with a messy memory: describe it (ADF) or measure its total effect and correct for it (PP). Neither is always better. Using both is cheap insurance.

    Something to think about

    Bill Phillips's MONIAC showed money flowing through an economy, like water. If you could add a tank to the MONIAC that never drains, what kind of time series would its water level be? A kite or a balloon? Leave your answer, and your favourite Phillips, in the comments.
