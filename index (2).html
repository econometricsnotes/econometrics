    The Test That Needed Its Own Table: Dickey and Fuller

    You want to know whether \(\rho = 1\). You know how to test whether a coefficient equals some number: run the regression, compute a t-statistic, look it up in the t-table at the back of the textbook. Simple.

    Except that here, the t-table at the back of the textbook is wrong. Not slightly wrong. Wrong enough that if you trust it, you will "discover" stationarity in almost half of all random walks. This chapter is about the two statisticians from Iowa who worked out what the right table looks like.

    A small trick with a big name

    Start from the AR(1) model of Chapter 1:

    \[ y_t = \rho\, y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \]

    Subtract \(y_{t-1}\) from both sides:

    \[ \underbrace{y_t - y_{t-1}}_{\Delta y_t} = (\rho - 1)\, y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \]

    Call \(\delta = \rho - 1\). Now the question "is \(\rho = 1\)?" becomes "is \(\delta = 0\)?":

    \[ \Delta y_t = \delta\, y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \qquad H_0: \delta = 0 \ (\text{unit root}) \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: \delta < 0 \ (\text{stationary}) \]

    Two things to notice:

    1. The test is one-sided. We only care about \(\delta < 0\). An explosive series with \(\rho > 1\), a balloon with a rocket attached, is not what economists usually worry about.
    2. The null hypothesis is the unit root. The test assumes "balloon" until the data prove otherwise. Keep this in mind; in Chapter 5 we will meet a test that does the exact opposite.

    Run the regression, compute \(t = \hat\delta / \operatorname{se}(\hat\delta)\), and compare it with a critical value. The whole drama is about which critical value.

    Why the t-table lies

    The ordinary t-test assumes the regressor behaves itself: it has a stable mean and variance. Under the null hypothesis, our regressor \(y_{t-1}\) is a random walk. Its variance grows with time, as we saw in Chapter 1. The usual theory breaks.

    I simulated 20,000 random walks of length 100. For each one I ran the Dickey-Fuller regression with a constant and recorded the t-statistic. In every one of them the true \(\delta\) is exactly zero. Here is the distribution of those t-statistics, against the normal curve the textbook assumes:

    The real distribution is shifted to the left and skewed. Its average is about −1.5, not 0. Its 5% point is −2.93, not −1.645.

    Now the scary number: 45% of these t-statistics are below −1.645. If you used the ordinary one-sided t-table at 5%, you would declare a random walk "stationary" in 45 cases out of 100. Your "5% test" would actually be a 45% test.

    Derivation: where the strange distribution comes from (click to open)

    Take the simplest case, no constant, \(y_0 = 0\), and true \(\rho = 1\). The OLS estimate is

    \[ \hat\rho - 1 = \frac{\sum y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t}{\sum y_{t-1}^2} \]

    Look at the numerator. Because \(y_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), squaring gives \(y_t^2 = y_{t-1}^2 + 2y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t + \varepsilon_t^2\), so

    \[ y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t = \tfrac12\left(y_t^2 - y_{t-1}^2 - \varepsilon_t^2\right) \]

    Add this up from \(t = 1\) to \(T\). The middle terms cancel like a telescope:

    \[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t = \tfrac12\left(y_T^2 - \sum_{t=1}^T \varepsilon_t^2\right) \]

    Divide by \(T\sigma^2\). Since \(y_T\) is a sum of \(T\) shocks, \(y_T/(\sigma\sqrt{T})\) is approximately standard normal, so \(y_T^2/(T\sigma^2)\) is approximately \(\chi^2_1\). And \(\sum\varepsilon_t^2/(T\sigma^2) \to 1\). Therefore

    \[ \frac{1}{T\sigma^2}\sum y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t \;\longrightarrow\; \tfrac12\left(\chi^2_1 - 1\right) \]

    A \(\chi^2_1\) variable has mean 1 but median only 0.45. So \(\chi^2_1 - 1\) is negative more than two-thirds of the time. That is the source of the leftward shift: the numerator of \(\hat\rho - 1\) is usually negative even when the truth is \(\rho = 1\).

    The denominator also does not settle down to a constant. In the language of advanced theory, both pieces converge to functionals of a Brownian motion \(W(r)\):

    \[ T(\hat\rho - 1) \Rightarrow \frac{\tfrac12\left(W(1)^2 - 1\right)}{\int_0^1 W(r)^2\,dr} \]

    Two more surprises hide in this formula. First, we multiply by \(T\), not \(\sqrt{T}\): the estimate of \(\rho\) converges to 1 faster than usual. Econometricians call this superconsistency. Second, the limit is a ratio of random quantities, not a normal distribution. No t-table can describe it, so someone had to simulate it.

    The two men from Iowa

    That someone was David Dickey, a PhD student at Iowa State University, and his supervisor, Wayne Fuller.

    Fuller is an unusual hero for a time-series story. He trained in agricultural economics at Iowa State, and he is best known as one of the giants of survey sampling: how to design and analyse surveys of farms, households and people. A conversation with him was published in the journal Survey Methodology in 2026, recounting his early life and his work, including the Dickey-Fuller test.

    In the 1970s there were no laptops. There was also no formula that would give the critical values directly. So Dickey and Fuller did what I did for the chart above, on the slow computers of the day: they generated a large number of artificial random walks, ran the regression on each, and tabulated the results. The critical values appeared in Fuller's 1976 book Introduction to Statistical Time Series, and the theory in two papers: Dickey and Fuller (1979) in the Journal of the American Statistical Association, and (1981) in Econometrica. They are among the most cited papers in statistics.

    David Dickey went on to a long career at North Carolina State University, where he is a William Neal Reynolds Professor of Statistics. Later, James MacKinnon of Queen's University in Canada used much larger simulations to fit formulas for the critical values at any sample size. That is why every EViews unit root output ends with the line "*MacKinnon (1996) one-sided p-values".

    Three versions of the test

    Real series rarely wander around zero. So the regression comes in three flavours:

    Version Regression Use when the series… 5% critical value (large samples)
    None \(\Delta y_t = \delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) wanders around zero −1.94
    Constant \(\Delta y_t = \alpha + \delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) wanders around a non-zero level −2.86
    Constant + trend \(\Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) has a clear upward or downward trend −3.41

    Every deterministic term you add pushes the critical value further left. The test has to work harder to prove stationarity, because a constant and a trend can themselves mimic some of the wandering.

    What does each version compare?
    • Constant: a random walk (with no drift) versus a stationary series around a fixed mean.
    • Constant + trend: a random walk with drift versus a stationary series around a straight-line trend. A random walk with drift, \(y_t = \mu + y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\), can be written \(y_t = y_0 + \mu t + \sum \varepsilon_s\): a trend plus a balloon. A trend-stationary series is \(y_t = a + bt + u_t\) with \(u_t\) a kite. Both trend upwards. Only one of them ever returns to its trend line.

    The data

    The sheet P10_dickey_fuller has 240 months of two simulated series:

    • EXRATE: an exchange rate in rupees per dollar. It is a random walk with a small drift (built to look like a currency that slowly weakens).
    • INFL: an inflation rate in percent. It is a stationary AR(1) with \(\rho = 0.75\) around 6%.

    EViews: open the series, View → Unit Root Tests → Standard Unit Root Test, choose "Augmented Dickey-Fuller", "Level", and set the lag length to a fixed 0. Zero lags makes ADF the plain Dickey-Fuller test.

    How to read it, in three steps:

    1. The test statistic is −2.74. That is just the t-statistic on EXRATE(-1) in the equation below.
    2. The 5% critical value is −3.43. Our statistic is not more negative than that.
    3. The p-value is 0.22. We cannot reject the unit root. The exchange rate looks like a balloon with a slow drift, which is exactly how I built it.

    Now spot the trap. In the test equation, EXRATE(-1) has a "Prob." of 0.0066. A careless reader shouts "significant!" and declares the series stationary. Never use the Prob. column of the test equation for the unit root coefficient. That number comes from the ordinary t-distribution, which is the very thing Dickey and Fuller showed does not apply. The only p-value that counts is the one at the top, 0.2205.

    Here the statistic is −6.14, far beyond the 1% critical value of −3.46. We reject the unit root: inflation is a kite.

    The coefficient tells us more. \(\hat\delta = -0.271\), so \(\hat\rho = 1 + \hat\delta = 0.729\), close to the true 0.75. The half-life of an inflation shock is \(\ln 0.5 / \ln 0.729 \approx 2.2\) months. A spike in inflation is half gone within about two months.

    And the constant? In a stationary series, \(\alpha = (1-\rho)\mu\), so the long-run mean is \(\hat\mu = \hat\alpha / (1-\hat\rho) = 1.591 / 0.271 \approx 5.9\%\). The kite's home is just under 6%.

    The lesson

    The Dickey-Fuller regression looks like any other regression, but it lives in a different world. The coefficient on \(y_{t-1}\) must be judged against Dickey-Fuller critical values, not against the t-table. A test statistic of −2.5 that would make you famous in an ordinary regression means nothing here.

    There is a catch that we have ignored so far: we assumed the gusts of wind \(\varepsilon_t\) are independent from one minute to the next. In real data they rarely are. Fixing that is the job of Chapter 3.

    Something to think about

    The test always starts from "unit root" and needs strong evidence to reject it. With only 30 years of annual data, what do you expect to happen when the true \(\rho\) is 0.9? Is "I could not reject a unit root" the same as "I found a unit root"? Answer in the comments; Chapter 6 has the numbers.
