    The Day the Rules Changed

    Some dates divide history into "before" and "after". For anyone in India, 8 November 2016 is one of them. Overnight, the old ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes stopped being legal tender, and millions of people who had never used a payments app suddenly had a reason to try one.

    About this post

    Concept: structural breaks, the Chow test and break dummies · Data sheet: P7_break · Simulated monthly data, 2012M01–2021M12, 120 observations

    Download the data (Excel) — sheet P7_break · EViews program

    This post is not about whether that policy was good or bad. It is about a quieter question every econometrician should ask: is the relationship I am estimating the same over the whole sample?

    The data

    DIGITAL is the percentage of retail payments in a town made digitally, recorded monthly. The numbers are simulated, but the shape will look familiar. Our only regressor is TREND (1 for 2012M01, up to 120 for 2021M12).

    A model that looks fine

    R-squared 0.92. A t-statistic of 37. The digital share grows by about 0.46 percentage points a month. Case closed?

    Not quite. That Durbin-Watson of 0.64 again tells us the residuals march in long runs rather than bouncing around zero. If you plot them (View → Actual, Fitted, Residual), you see a smile: residuals are positive in 2012–13, sink to their deepest just before November 2016, stay negative into 2018, and turn positive again from 2019. One straight line is being forced through two different stories.

    The Chow test

    Gregory Chow's idea (1960) is beautifully simple. Fit the model separately on the "before" and "after" samples. If the world did not change, the two separate fits should do no better than the single fit. If they do much better, the relationship broke.

    In EViews: View → Stability Diagnostics → Chow Breakpoint Test, and enter 2016M11.

    Chow Breakpoint Test: 2016M11
Null Hypothesis: No breaks at specified breakpoints
Varying regressors: All equation variables
Equation Sample: 2012M01 2021M12

F-statistic               137.2203    Prob. F(2,116)                    0.0000
Log likelihood ratio      145.6423    Prob. Chi-Square(2)               0.0000
Wald Statistic            274.4407    Prob. Chi-Square(2)               0.0000
==============================================================================

    An F-statistic of 137 with a p-value of 0.0000. The null of "no break" is firmly rejected. The F has 2 numerator degrees of freedom because two things were allowed to change: the intercept and the slope.

    Model the break instead of ignoring it

    Create two new variables: series post = (trend>=59), which is 1 from November 2016 onward, and use post*(trend-59) so the new slope starts counting from the break.

    Now the story is clear:

    Period Monthly growth in digital share What changed
    Before Nov 2016 0.15 points a month Slow, steady adoption
    At Nov 2016 One-time jump of 7.2 points The shock itself
    After Nov 2016 0.15 + 0.42 = 0.57 points a month Adoption almost four times faster

    The single-trend model said 0.46 points a month throughout. That number described no actual period in the data. It was an average of a slow world and a fast world. The Durbin-Watson is now 2.05, and the standard error of the regression has nearly halved, from 4.72 to 2.60.

    Two cautions

    1. Know your date before you look. The Chow test assumes you picked the break date for a reason outside the data. If you scan every possible date and pick the one with the biggest F, the usual p-values are far too optimistic. For an unknown date, use the Quandt-Andrews test (View → Stability Diagnostics → Quandt-Andrews Breakpoint Test) or the Bai-Perron multiple breakpoint tests.
    2. A break is a fact, not an explanation. The test tells you something changed in November 2016. It does not tell you why. Smartphone prices, a new app or a festival season could all line up with the same date.

    The lesson

    A regression that fits beautifully over ten years can still be wrong about every single one of them. Before trusting a slope, ask whether the world stayed the same long enough for one slope to make sense.

    Something to think about

    The COVID-19 lockdown began in March 2020, inside our sample. Should we test for a second break there? If you find one, how would you decide whether it is a permanent change or a temporary blip?
