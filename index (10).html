    Twenty States, One Question: Panel Unit Root Tests (and a Field Guide to All of Them)

    Chapter 6 ended with a sad fact: with 30 years of annual data, even a good unit root test is half blind. You can't make history longer. But you can look at more places. If you have 30 years of data for 20 states, you have 600 observations of the same economic process playing out in 20 slightly different settings. Can we pool them to see better?

    Yes, with care. This final chapter is about panel unit root tests, the tricky questions they answer, and a field guide to every test in this series.

    The data first

    The sheet P16_panel has annual inflation for 20 imaginary states, S01 to S20, from 1994 to 2023. I built 17 of them as stationary AR(1) series with \(\rho\) between 0.2 and 0.6. I built the other three, S06, S13 and S18, as random walks.

    Can you spot the three random walks without the red colour? It's not easy. S06 drifts up and stays up for a decade; the others are subtle.

    Pooling: the idea and the catch

    Pooling the states gives the test far more information. But it forces us to think hard about a question that single-series tests never raise: what exactly is the alternative hypothesis?

    • "All 20 states are stationary"?
    • "At least some states are stationary"?
    • "All states share the same \(\rho\)"?

    Different tests answer different questions. Getting this wrong is the most common mistake with panel unit root tests.

    The main panel tests

    Levin, Lin and Chu (2002): one \(\rho\) for everybody

    The LLC test runs the ADF regression for every state but forces a common autoregressive coefficient:

    \[ \Delta y_{it} = \alpha_i + \delta\, y_{i,t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p_i} \gamma_{ij}\, \Delta y_{i,t-j} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

    Each state has its own constant \(\alpha_i\) and its own lags, but one \(\delta\). The hypotheses are \(H_0: \delta = 0\) for all states versus \(H_1: \delta < 0\) for all states. Breitung's (2000) test makes the same assumption with a different correction for bias.

    The catch: is it believable that the inflation persistence in Kerala and Punjab is exactly the same? Usually not. That's why the next test is more popular.

    Im, Pesaran and Shin (2003): everyone gets their own \(\rho\)

    IPS let each state have its own \(\delta_i\). They simply run a separate ADF test for each state, then average the t-statistics:

    \[ \bar t = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} t_i \]

    Under the null, every \(t_i\) has a Dickey-Fuller distribution with a known mean and variance (they depend on the sample length and the number of lags). Standardise the average and you get

    \[ W_{\bar t} = \frac{\sqrt{N}\,\left(\bar t - \operatorname{E}[t_i]\right)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[t_i]}} \;\sim\; N(0,1) \]

    For \(T = 30\) and no lags, \(\operatorname{E}[t_i] \approx -1.52\) and \(\operatorname{Var}[t_i] \approx 0.80\). IPS tabulated these by simulation, much as Dickey and Fuller had done for a single series.

    The hypotheses: \(H_0\): every state has a unit root. \(H_1\): some states are stationary. Rejecting the null does not mean all states are stationary.

    M. Hashem Pesaran, the P in IPS, has taught at Cambridge and the University of Southern California, and he is one of the most cited econometricians alive. He comes back below.

    Fisher tests (Maddala and Wu 1999; Choi 2001): combine the p-values

    An even simpler idea, borrowed from R. A. Fisher's work in the 1930s on combining independent experiments. Run any unit root test on each state, take its p-value \(p_i\), and combine:

    \[ P = -2\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ln p_i \;\sim\; \chi^2_{2N} \]
    Why does this work? (click to open)

    If the null is true, each p-value is uniformly distributed between 0 and 1. For a uniform \(p\), \(-2\ln p\) has a \(\chi^2_2\) distribution. Adding \(N\) independent \(\chi^2_2\) variables gives \(\chi^2_{2N}\). Small p-values make \(-\ln p_i\) big, so many small p-values make \(P\) big.

    EViews reports the Fisher test with ADF p-values ("ADF - Fisher Chi-square") and with Phillips-Perron p-values ("PP - Fisher Chi-square"), plus Choi's version \(Z = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum \Phi^{-1}(p_i)\), which is normal under the null. Like IPS, the alternative is "some states are stationary".

    Hadri (2000): KPSS for panels

    Hadri takes the KPSS idea of Chapter 5 to panels. Compute the KPSS statistic \(\eta_i\) for each state, average them, and standardise:

    \[ Z = \frac{\sqrt{N}\,\left(\bar\eta - \tfrac16\right)}{\sqrt{1/45}} \;\sim\; N(0,1) \]

    (With a constant only, each KPSS statistic has mean 1/6 and variance 1/45 under the null.) The hypotheses are turned around: \(H_0\): every state is stationary. \(H_1\): some states have a unit root.

    Wait, both nulls rejected?

    Yes, and both tests are right.

    • IPS rejects "all 20 states have a unit root". True: 17 of them don't.
    • Hadri rejects "all 20 states are stationary". Also true: 3 of them aren't.

    The truth sits between the two nulls, and both tests have found it. What neither test can tell you is which states are which, or how many. For that you are back to looking state by state, with all the low power that implies.

    This example shows the most important rule for panel unit root tests: state the hypotheses in words before you interpret the result. "The panel is stationary" is almost never the right conclusion. "Not every state has a unit root" usually is.

    Two more problems: neighbours and seasons

    States are not independent. A national monetary policy, an oil price shock or a monsoon failure hits many states at once. All the tests above assume the states' shocks are independent, and they can reject far too often when they are not. Pesaran (2007) proposed the CIPS test, which adds cross-section averages to each regression to soak up common shocks. Tests like this are called "second generation" panel unit root tests. Whenever your panel units share a common economy, use one.

    Seasons can have unit roots too. Monthly or quarterly data can wander at seasonal frequencies: the monsoon pattern itself might drift over the years. Hylleberg, Engle, Granger and Yoo (1990) developed the HEGY test for seasonal unit roots. Two of its four authors, Robert Engle and Clive Granger, shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Economics: Engle for modelling time-varying volatility (ARCH), Granger for cointegration. Not many papers on seasonal adjustment can claim two Nobel laureates.

    And the five rules I would pin above every student's desk:

    1. Plot the series first. Your eyes pick the deterministic terms and spot breaks.
    2. "Cannot reject" is not "found". Unit root tests have low power. Use DF-GLS or Ng-Perron near the boundary.
    3. Use two courts. Pair a unit-root-null test with a stationarity-null test.
    4. Look for steps in the road. One break can turn a kite into a balloon in the eyes of the test.
    5. Say the hypotheses in words, especially with panels.

    Where next?

    Remember Pearson's drunk from Chapter 1, wandering away from his lamp post? In 1994 Michael Murray wrote a short paper in The American Statistician called "A drunk and her dog". The drunk wanders. Her dog wanders. But every time the dog strays too far, she calls its name, and it comes back towards her. Both are random walks, yet the distance between them is a kite.

    That is cointegration, Granger's Nobel-winning idea, and it is where this blog goes next.

    Something to think about

    A study runs the IPS test on real exchange rates for 25 countries, rejects the null, and concludes "purchasing power parity holds for all countries". Write a two-sentence comment explaining what the test really showed, and one extra test you would ask them to run. Leave it in the comments.
