    R-squared Is Not a Report Card

    Every econometrics teacher has met this student. They run a regression, look straight at the R-squared, and feel either proud or ashamed. 0.9 is an A+. 0.2 is a fail.

    About this post

    Concept: R-squared, adjusted R-squared and information criteria · Data sheet: P3_rsquared · Simulated cross-section, 40 students

    Download the data (Excel) — sheet P3_rsquared · EViews program

    This post is for that student.

    A simple, honest model

    Now let's "improve" it

    Our ambitious student wants a higher R-squared. So they add four more variables:

    • SHOE — shoe size
    • BMONTH — month of birth (1 to 12)
    • LETTERS — number of letters in the student's first name
    • BUSWAIT — minutes spent waiting for the bus on exam morning

    None of these has anything to do with the score. I generated them as pure noise.

    The R-squared went up, from 0.682 to 0.699. Shoe size and bus waiting time "explained" more of the exam scores!

    Why R-squared can only go up

    R-squared measures how much of the variation in Y the fitted line covers. Adding any variable, even random noise, gives OLS one more knob to turn. OLS will always use it to shave off a little more of the sum of squared residuals, which fell here from 2156.3 to 2041.8. It can never make the fit worse. So R-squared never falls when you add a regressor.

    That is why R-squared is a poor judge of whether a variable belongs in the model.

    The fairer judges

    Look at the other lines in the two tables.

    Measure HOURS only HOURS + 4 junk variables Better model
    R-squared 0.682 0.699 Looks like junk wins
    Adjusted R-squared 0.674 0.655 HOURS only
    S.E. of regression 7.53 7.75 HOURS only
    Akaike info criterion 6.925 7.071 HOURS only
    Schwarz criterion 7.010 7.324 HOURS only

    Adjusted R-squared charges a fee for every extra variable. Akaike and Schwarz do the same (smaller is better), and Schwarz charges the highest fee. Every one of them prefers the simple model.

    The most direct check is to test whether the four junk variables matter jointly. In the equation window: View → Coefficient Diagnostics → Wald Test, and enter c(3)=0, c(4)=0, c(5)=0, c(6)=0.

    A p-value of 0.75. The data see no reason to keep any of them.

    The other half of the story: a low R-squared can be perfectly fine

    What if the R-squared were 0.15? Many students would panic. They should not. Human behaviour is noisy. If you want to know how much an extra hour of study is worth, what matters is whether the slope is estimated without bias and with reasonable precision. A model can capture a true, important effect and still leave most of the variation unexplained, because many other things (sleep, luck, the questions set) also move exam scores. You will see a real example in Post 4, where the correct model has an R-squared of only 0.38.

    And a very high R-squared can be a warning sign rather than a medal. Post 2 showed an R-squared of 0.72 between two series that had nothing to do with each other.

    The lesson

    R-squared answers one narrow question: how closely does the line hug the data in this sample? It does not tell you whether the model is right, whether the coefficients are unbiased, or whether the model will predict well tomorrow.

    Something to think about

    Model A explains house prices with R-squared = 0.90 using 25 variables and 60 houses. Model B explains them with R-squared = 0.70 using 3 variables. You must predict the price of the next 100 houses that come on the market. Which model do you trust, and why?
