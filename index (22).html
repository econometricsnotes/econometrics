    Giving the Test a Memory: The Augmented Dickey-Fuller Test

    The plain Dickey-Fuller test makes one heroic assumption: the shocks that push the series around are completely unpredictable, like coin tosses. Real economies are not like that. A good quarter for business tends to be followed by another good quarter. Monsoon effects spill over several months. If yesterday's shock tells you something about today's, the Dickey-Fuller test is reading the wrong table again.

    The cure, invented by Said E. Said and David Dickey, is almost embarrassingly simple: give the regression a memory.

    Where the extra terms come from

    Suppose the series depends on its last two values, an AR(2):

    \[ y_t = \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t \]

    Let's rewrite it with the same trick as in Chapter 2. Add and subtract \(\phi_2 y_{t-1}\) on the right-hand side:

    \[ y_t = (\phi_1 + \phi_2)\, y_{t-1} - \phi_2 (y_{t-1} - y_{t-2}) + \varepsilon_t \]

    Now subtract \(y_{t-1}\) from both sides:

    \[ \Delta y_t = \underbrace{(\phi_1 + \phi_2 - 1)}_{\delta}\, y_{t-1} \;\underbrace{-\,\phi_2}_{\gamma_1}\, \Delta y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \]

    This is the Dickey-Fuller regression with one extra term, the lagged change \(\Delta y_{t-1}\). And the unit root question is still the question "is \(\delta = 0\)?", because an AR(2) has a unit root exactly when \(\phi_1 + \phi_2 = 1\).

    Why does \(\phi_1 + \phi_2 = 1\) mean a unit root?

    The lag polynomial of an AR(2) is \(1 - \phi_1 z - \phi_2 z^2\). Put \(z = 1\): you get \(1 - \phi_1 - \phi_2\). If this is zero, then \(z = 1\) is a root of the polynomial. A unit root, by definition.

    The same algebra works for any number of lags. The Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) regression is

    \[ \Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \delta\, y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \gamma_j\, \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t \]

    The lagged changes soak up the short-run memory. What is left in \(\varepsilon_t\) is, we hope, pure noise again, and the t-statistic on \(\delta\) follows the same Dickey-Fuller distribution as before. That is the beauty of it: no new tables needed.

    A man named Said

    In 1984 the journal Biometrika published "Testing for unit roots in autoregressive-moving average models of unknown order" by Said E. Said and David A. Dickey. They showed that even if the true process is more complicated than any finite AR, with moving-average parts as well, the ADF regression still works as long as the number of lags grows slowly with the sample size.

    So the ADF test is, in a sense, what Said said. (Every econometrics teacher has made this joke. I could not resist either.)

    How many lags? The Goldilocks problem

    Choosing \(p\) is the one real decision in an ADF test.

    • Too few lags: leftover autocorrelation in the residuals. The test statistic no longer follows the Dickey-Fuller distribution, and the test rejects too often or too rarely.
    • Too many lags: each extra lag costs a degree of freedom and blurs the estimate of \(\delta\). The test loses power, its ability to spot a kite.

    The usual approach is to let an information criterion choose. EViews starts from a maximum lag given by Schwert's rule

    \[ p_{\max} = \left\lfloor 12 \left(\frac{T}{100}\right)^{1/4} \right\rfloor \]

    and picks the lag between 0 and \(p_{\max}\) with the smallest Schwarz Information Criterion (SIC). For 120 quarterly observations, \(p_{\max} = \lfloor 12 \times 1.2^{0.25}\rfloor = 12\). Ng and Perron (2001) proposed a "modified" criterion (MAIC) that behaves better when the series is close to a unit root; EViews offers it as an option.

    Which deterministic terms?

    The second decision is whether to include a constant, or a constant and a trend. The rule of thumb is: look at the picture first. The test should be able to describe the series under both hypotheses.

    A more formal route, the sequential procedure of Dolado, Jenkinson and Sosvilla-Rivero (1990), starts with the most general model and tests whether the trend can be dropped. In practice, the picture usually settles it.

    The data

    The sheet P11_adf has 30 years of quarterly data on LGDP, the natural log of real GDP for an imaginary economy. I built it as a random walk with drift: quarterly growth averages 1.5%, and growth itself is autocorrelated (a good quarter tends to be followed by another one, with \(\rho = 0.45\)). In truth, LGDP is I(1) and its growth rate is I(0).

    Read it from the top:

    • Lag Length: 1 chosen by SIC, out of a maximum of 12.
    • D(LGDP(-1)) has a coefficient of 0.40 with a t-statistic of 4.7. That is the memory in growth rates (the true value is 0.45). This coefficient can be judged with ordinary t-tests: the lagged differences are stationary, so their t-statistics are well behaved.
    • The Durbin-Watson stat is now 2.16. The residuals look like noise. Good.
    • The ADF statistic is −1.87, against a 5% critical value of −3.45. p = 0.67. We cannot reject the unit root.

    Also notice the coefficient on LGDP(-1): −0.037. Even in a "unit root" series, OLS rarely gives exactly zero. And remember Chapter 2's warning: its "Prob." of 0.0646 in the equation table is meaningless for this purpose.

    Step 3: test the first difference

    If LGDP is I(1), its first difference, the quarterly growth rate, should be stationary. Growth has no trend, so a constant is enough (command: lgdp.uroot(adf, const, dif=1), or choose "1st difference" in the dialog).

Exogenous: Constant

    −7.35 against −2.89. A clear rejection. The growth rate is a kite.

    Conclusion: LGDP has one unit root; it is I(1). In plain words: a shock to this economy's GDP never fully goes away, but its growth rate always comes back to its average of about 1.5% a quarter.

    A note on "the trend is significant"

    Students often see that @TREND has a t-statistic of 1.85 and conclude something about the trend. Careful: in the ADF equation, the constant and trend coefficients also have non-standard distributions under the null. Dickey and Fuller (1981) tabulated separate critical values for them. As a rule, use the test equation to check the residuals and the lag terms, and use only the top half of the output for the unit root decision.

    The lesson

    The ADF test is the Dickey-Fuller test with a memory. Add enough lagged changes to clean up the residuals, but not so many that you blind the test. Then always ask the follow-up question: if the level has a unit root, is the first difference stationary?

    Something to think about

    Take a monthly series with a strong seasonal pattern, such as umbrella sales from Post 6. What would SIC need to do with the lags to capture a 12-month pattern? What could go wrong if the maximum lag were only 4? Tell me in the comments.
