    When Two Random Walks Fall in Love

    A cinema owner keeps a weekly index of popcorn sales. Across town, a pet shop keeps a weekly index of goldfish sold. The two businesses have never heard of each other. No customer links them. No supplier links them.

    About this post

    Concept: spurious regression and non-stationarity · Data sheet: P2_spurious · Simulated weekly data, 200 observations

    Download the data (Excel) — sheet P2_spurious · EViews program

    A t-statistic of 22. An R-squared of 0.72. On paper, each extra point of goldfish sales comes with 1.35 points of popcorn sales. A consultant could build a whole PowerPoint deck on "cross-category demand synergies".

    But I built these two series myself, completely independently. Each one is a random walk: this week's value is last week's value plus a random shock.

    \[ POPCORN_t = POPCORN_{t-1} + e_t \qquad GOLDFISH_t = GOLDFISH_{t-1} + v_t \]

    The shocks e and v have nothing to do with each other. The true slope is exactly zero.

    Why does this happen?

    A random walk wanders. It has no home to return to. Over 200 weeks, it drifts up for a while, then down, then sideways, purely by chance. Two such wanderers will very often drift in the same direction for long stretches. OLS sees those long shared drifts and calls them a relationship.

    The usual t-test assumes the data settle around a stable mean (they are stationary). Random walks do not. With them, the t-statistic does not follow a t distribution at all; it tends to grow as the sample gets longer. More data makes the illusion stronger, not weaker. This is the famous result of Granger and Newbold (1974), later explained in theory by Phillips (1986).

    The smoking gun in the output

    Look at the bottom right corner: Durbin-Watson stat = 0.137. A DW near 2 means the residuals behave like fresh noise. A DW near 0 means each residual is almost a copy of the last one. The residuals are wandering too.

    Granger and Newbold gave a handy rule of thumb: if the R-squared is bigger than the Durbin-Watson statistic, be suspicious. Here 0.72 is more than five times 0.137.

    The cure: look at changes, not levels

    If the two series are truly unrelated, then this week's change in goldfish sales should tell us nothing about this week's change in popcorn sales.

    ls d(popcorn) c d(goldfish)

    The romance is over. The slope is essentially zero (p = 0.95), the R-squared is 0.00002, and the Durbin-Watson statistic is back near 2.

    A word of caution

    Differencing is not always the answer. Sometimes two trending series really are tied together over the long run, such as income and consumption. Then they are cointegrated, and throwing away the levels throws away the most interesting information. Before you regress levels on levels, test each series for a unit root (in EViews: open the series, then View → Unit Root Tests). If both have unit roots, test whether they are cointegrated before you believe the levels regression.

    Something to think about

    Global average temperature and the number of countries with a mobile phone network both trended upward for decades. Suppose someone regresses one on the other and finds R-squared = 0.95. What two checks would you ask for before reading anything into it?
