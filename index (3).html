    When the Errors Fan Out

    Ask a family earning ₹20,000 a month how much they spend on food, and the answer will sit in a narrow range. There is only so much room to move. Ask a family earning ₹1,50,000 a month, and the answers spread out. One family cooks simple meals at home; another eats out four nights a week. Same income, very different food bills.

    About this post

    Concept: heteroskedasticity, the White test and robust standard errors · Data sheet: P5_hetero · Simulated cross-section, 200 households

    Download the data (Excel) — sheet P5_hetero · EViews program

    That simple observation has a long name: heteroskedasticity. It means the spread of the errors is not the same for everyone.

    The data

    For 200 households we have monthly FOOD spending and monthly INCOME, both in ₹ thousand.

    Out of every extra ₹1,000 of income, about ₹221 goes on food. That is a sensible marginal propensity to spend on food, and close to the 0.22 I used to create the data.

    So far, so good. But the table has a hidden assumption. Every standard error above is computed as if the errors have the same variance for every household. We have just argued they don't.

    Picture it first

    In EViews, open the equation and choose View → Actual, Fitted, Residual → Residual Graph, or make a scatter of the residuals against INCOME. You will see a fan or a trumpet: residuals huddle close to zero for poorer households and spread wide for richer ones.

    Then test it

    View → Residual Diagnostics → Heteroskedasticity Tests → White.

    Heteroskedasticity Test: White
Null hypothesis: Homoskedasticity

==============================================================================
F-statistic               23.09812    Prob. F(2,197)                    0.0000
Obs*R-squared             37.99092    Prob. Chi-Square(2)               0.0000
Scaled explained SS       70.91685    Prob. Chi-Square(2)               0.0000
==============================================================================

    The idea behind the White test is easy. Take the squared residuals, which are rough measures of each household's error variance. Regress them on INCOME and INCOME squared. If income helps predict how big the errors are, the variance is not constant. Here it clearly does: all three p-values are 0.0000. We reject homoskedasticity.

    Does it matter?

    Here is the good news, which surprises many students: the coefficients are still unbiased. Heteroskedasticity does not move the line. The bad news: the usual standard errors are wrong, so the t-statistics and p-values cannot be trusted.

    Fix 1: robust standard errors

    Keep the same coefficients, but compute standard errors that allow the variance to differ across households. In EViews, under Options choose Covariance method: White, or type ls(cov=white) food c income.

    Compare the two tables line by line. The coefficients are identical. The R-squared is identical. Only the standard errors changed: the one on INCOME rose from 0.0094 to 0.0105, and the one on C fell. Robust standard errors do not always get bigger. They get honest.

    Fix 2: change the scale

    Often the fan exists because the relationship is proportional. A rich family's errors are big in rupees but similar in percentages. Taking logs puts everyone on a percentage scale. ls log(food) c log(income)

    The White test no longer rejects at 5%. As a bonus, the slope now has a neat meaning: a 1% rise in income raises food spending by about 0.73%. Food is a necessity (elasticity below 1), just as Ernst Engel noticed in the 1850s.

    The lesson

    Heteroskedasticity is not a disaster; it is information. It tells you the world is more varied at one end than at the other. Ignore it and your p-values lie. Notice it and you often discover a better way to write the model.

    Something to think about

    In the log model, the constant has a p-value of 0.29. Should we drop it? What would forcing the line through the origin mean economically for a family with an income of ₹1,000 a month?
