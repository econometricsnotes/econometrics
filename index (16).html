    Two Friends Who Always Arrive Together

    Anyone who has hunted for a flat in an Indian city knows the two numbers on every listing: built-up area and carpet area. Built-up area includes the walls and balcony; carpet area is the floor you can actually lay a carpet on. Carpet area is usually around three-quarters to four-fifths of built-up area.

    About this post

    Concept: multicollinearity, VIFs and joint tests · Data sheet: P8_multicol · Simulated cross-section, 60 flats

    Download the data (Excel) — sheet P8_multicol · EViews program

    Both surely matter for the rent. So let's put both in a regression and see which one matters more.

    The data

    Sixty flats. For each: monthly RENT (₹ thousand), BUILTUP area and CARPET area (both in sq ft).

    ls rent c builtup carpet

    A genuine puzzle. Look at the two t-statistics: 0.42 and 0.38. Neither size measure is anywhere near significant. By the usual reading, the size of a flat does not affect its rent.

    Now look at the bottom of the same table. The F-statistic is 81.6 with a p-value of 0.000000, and the R-squared is 0.74. The model as a whole explains rent very well.

    So the regression is saying: "Size matters enormously. I just can't tell you which measure of size."

    What's going on

    BUILTUP and CARPET move together almost perfectly. In these data their correlation is 0.998. To separate their effects, OLS needs flats where one is large and the other is small. There are hardly any such flats. When two regressors always arrive together, the data cannot say which one deserves the credit. That is multicollinearity.

    Think of two friends who always come to your party together. Your party is always livelier when they come. But is it him or her? You will never know unless one of them turns up alone.

==============================================================================

    Read the Centered VIF column. A VIF of 257 means the variance of each slope is about 257 times bigger than it would be if the two regressors were unrelated. So each standard error is about √257 ≈ 16 times bigger. A common rule of thumb flags VIFs above 10. We are far beyond that.

    Asking the right question: a joint test

    The individual t-tests ask, "Does BUILTUP matter, holding CARPET fixed?" That is a strange question; in real life you almost never change one without the other. The sensible question is, "Does size matter at all?" Test both slopes together. View → Coefficient Diagnostics → Wald Test, with c(2)=0, c(3)=0.

    Wald Test:
Equation: EQ01

==============================================================================
Test Statistic                 Value              df     Probability
==============================================================================
F-statistic                 81.61314         (2, 57)          0.0000
Chi-square                  163.2263               2          0.0000
==============================================================================

    Emphatically yes. Together, the two size variables are hugely significant. Each one on its own looked useless only because it was sharing the credit with its twin.

    One sensible fix

    Keep one measure of size. ls rent c builtup

    The standard error drops from 0.0164 to 0.0010, and the t-statistic leaps to 12.9. The R-squared barely moves (0.741 to 0.741). An extra 100 sq ft of built-up area adds about ₹1,300 a month to the rent. Both Akaike and Schwarz prefer this simpler model.

    But be honest about what this coefficient now means. It is the effect of built-up area together with the carpet area that comes with it. It is not the effect of adding walls while holding the floor fixed. Dropping CARPET is fine for predicting rent. It would be wrong to claim we have isolated the separate value of a wall. Notice the link to Post 1: dropping a correlated variable always shifts its effect onto the one you keep.

    Things that do not fix multicollinearity

    • Removing a variable just because its t-statistic is small, when theory says it belongs and it is not a near-duplicate.
    • Hoping that a fancier estimation method will create information the data do not contain.

    What does help: more data with genuine variation (old buildings with thick walls, new ones with thin walls), combining the variables into one index that makes sense, or using outside information about one of the coefficients.

    The lesson

    Multicollinearity is not a mistake in your model. It is a shortage of information in your data. The coefficients are still unbiased; they are just blurry. Insignificant t-statistics alongside a highly significant F-statistic is its signature.

    Something to think about

    A researcher regresses a child's test score on both the mother's years of schooling and the father's years of schooling. Neither is significant, but the F-test is. A journalist writes: "Study finds parents' education doesn't matter." Write a two-sentence letter to the editor explaining what went wrong.
