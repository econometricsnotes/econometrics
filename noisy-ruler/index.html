    The Noisy Ruler

    Ask a student how many hours they slept last night and you will get an answer. You will not necessarily get the truth. People round, forget, guess, and sometimes tell you what sounds respectable.

    About this post

    Concept: measurement error and attenuation bias · Data sheet: P4_measurement · Simulated cross-section, 150 students

    Download the data (Excel) — sheet P4_measurement · EViews program

    What happens to a regression when the X variable is measured with a noisy ruler?

    Two rulers, one truth

    A college runs a small study on 150 students. For each student we have:

    • MARKS — the end-of-term mark
    • SLEEP_TRACKER — average nightly sleep in hours, measured by a wrist tracker (accurate)
    • SLEEP_SELF — average nightly sleep the student says they get, rounded to the nearest half hour (noisy)

    The true rule I used to build the data: every extra hour of sleep adds 4 marks.

    The effect of sleep has shrunk from about 4 to about 2.3. Still "highly significant", so nobody reading the table would notice a problem. But the estimate is wrong by more than 40%.

    Why the slope shrinks

    The self-reported number is the truth plus some noise:

    \[ SLEEP\_SELF = SLEEP\_TRUE + error \]

    Part of the variation in SLEEP_SELF is real differences in sleep, which do move marks. The rest is reporting noise, which moves nothing. OLS cannot tell the two apart. It averages a real effect (4) with a fake one (0), and the result is pulled towards zero.

    How much shrinkage? Roughly by the share of the variance that is real:

    \[ \text{reliability} = \frac{Var(SLEEP\_TRUE)}{Var(SLEEP\_SELF)} \]

    In these data the tracker's variance is 1.08 and the self-report's variance is 1.74, so the reliability is about 0.62. Multiply the true effect by it: 4 × 0.62 ≈ 2.5, close to what we got. Econometricians call this attenuation bias: noise in X attenuates, or weakens, the estimated effect.

    A twist: noise in Y is far less harmful

    If the marks had been recorded with random errors instead, the slope would still be unbiased. The extra noise would simply land in the error term, making the standard errors bigger and the R-squared smaller. Noise in Y costs you precision. Noise in X costs you the right answer.

    What can we do?

    1. Measure better. The tracker data are the best fix of all.
    2. Use an instrument. A second, independent noisy measure (say, a roommate's report of the student's sleep) can serve as an instrumental variable. In EViews: Quick → Estimate Equation → Method: TSLS.
    3. Remember the direction. With classical noise in a single regressor, the true effect is probably larger than your estimate. That alone can change a policy conclusion.

    The lesson

    A small, significant coefficient does not prove the effect is small. It may be a big effect seen through a foggy window.

    Something to think about

    Surveys often find that the measured return to an extra year of schooling is modest. Years of schooling are usually self-reported. Using this post, which way would that bias the estimate? Now think of a reason why the bias could go the other way.
