    The Road with a Step: How the Great Crash Rescued the Trend

    In 1982 two economists published a paper that shook macroeconomics. Charles Nelson and Charles Plosser took 14 long annual US series, including GNP, industrial production, employment, prices, wages and interest rates, and ran Dickey-Fuller tests on all of them. For all but one, the unemployment rate, they could not reject a unit root.

    The conclusion seemed to follow: recessions are not temporary dips below a trend. They are permanent. The economy is a balloon, not a kite. Seven years later, a young Canadian econometrician named Pierre Perron pointed at two dates in history and said: not so fast.

    Why Nelson and Plosser mattered

    If output is trend-stationary, a recession is a kite in a gust: painful, but the economy returns to its old path. Policy can smooth the ride. If output has a unit root, a recession permanently lowers the path. Part of every shock lasts for ever.

    Nelson and Plosser's result was taken as strong support for real business cycle theory, in which fluctuations are driven largely by permanent shocks to technology. The paper, "Trends and random walks in macroeconomic time series" (Journal of Monetary Economics, 1982), became one of the most cited in the field. Charles Plosser later served as President of the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia from 2006 to 2015. Few econometric papers end with an author helping to set American interest rates.

    Perron's objection: the Great Crash and the oil shock

    Pierre Perron's paper, "The Great Crash, the Oil Price Shock, and the Unit Root Hypothesis" (Econometrica, 1989), made a simple argument.

    Suppose a series is a perfectly good kite around a trend, except that once, the trend itself shifted. The string was moved to a new peg. A Dickey-Fuller test fits a single straight trend through the whole sample. After the shift, the series never returns to that line, so the test concludes: "this series does not come back to its trend; it must be a random walk."

    Perron treated two events as one-off breaks: the 1929 crash, which lowered the level of most series, and the 1973 oil price shock, which slowed post-war growth. Once he allowed for those breaks, he could reject the unit root for 11 of the 14 Nelson-Plosser series. The economy was a kite after all, just one whose string had been moved twice in a century.

    Three kinds of break

    Perron described three models. Write \(T_B\) for the break date, and define two dummy variables:

    \[ DU_t = \begin{cases} 1 & t > T_B \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad DT_t = \begin{cases} t - T_B & t > T_B \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

    \(DU_t\) shifts the level of the trend. \(DT_t\) changes its slope. These are the dummy variables of Post 6 again, now moving a trend instead of a seasonal pattern.

    Model What changes at the break Extra terms
    A, the "crash" model The level jumps \(DU_t\)
    B, the "changing growth" model The slope changes \(DT_t\)
    C, both Level and slope \(DU_t\) and \(DT_t\)

    The test regression for model C looks like an ADF regression with the break terms added:

    \[ \Delta y_t = \mu + \beta t + \theta\, DU_t + \gamma\, DT_t + \delta\, y_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} c_j\, \Delta y_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t \]

    The unit root test is still the t-statistic on \(\delta\), but with new critical values, because the break terms change the distribution.

    Zivot and Andrews: don't peek at the date

    Perron's critics, Lawrence Christiano among them, raised a fair point. Perron chose 1929 and 1973 after looking at the data. If you get to choose the break after seeing where the series jumps, you will find "evidence" of a break far too easily. It is like drawing the target after shooting the arrow.

    Eric Zivot and Donald Andrews answered in 1992, in a paper titled "Further evidence on the great crash, the oil-price shock, and the unit-root hypothesis" (Journal of Business & Economic Statistics). Their fix: let the data pick the break date, and account for the picking in the critical values.

    1. Try every possible break date, leaving out the first and last 15% of the sample.
    2. For each date, run the regression above and record the t-statistic on \(\delta\).
    3. Choose the date where the t-statistic is most negative: the date most favourable to stationarity.
    4. Compare that minimum t-statistic with critical values that allow for the search.

    Because we search for the best date, the critical values are more demanding than Perron's. For model C, they are −5.57 at 1%, −5.08 at 5% and −4.82 at 10%. The null hypothesis is a unit root without a break; the alternative is a trend-stationary series with one break at an unknown date.

    When Zivot and Andrews re-examined the Nelson-Plosser data this way, fewer series rejected the unit root than in Perron's study. Science is a conversation.

    The data

    The sheet P15_break has LINCOME, the log of real income per person in an imaginary state, Swarnapur, from 1960 to 2023. I built it as a trend-stationary series (shocks have \(\rho = 0.55\)) with growth of about 1.8% a year. In 1991, an economic reform raises the level of income and lifts the growth rate to about 4.8% a year. There is no unit root anywhere in the true process.

    The red dashed line is what an ordinary ADF test with a trend has in mind. The series sits above it until the mid-1970s, falls below it from the late 1970s to the early 2000s, and rises above it again at the end. To the ADF test this looks like wandering. The blue line, with a break in 1991, fits like a glove.

    The Zivot-Andrews test

    In EViews 9 or later, open the series and choose View → Unit Root Tests → Breakpoint Unit Root Test, with "Trend and intercept" for both the trend and the break, "Innovational outlier" as the break type, and "Minimize Dickey-Fuller t-statistic" to pick the date. Older versions offer the Zivot-Andrews test through a free add-in. The output below is in the Zivot-Andrews layout; the built-in breakpoint test finds the same break date but reports slightly different critical values, because it uses Vogelsang's (1993) tables.

    Zivot-Andrews Unit Root Test
Date: 10/27/26   Time: 21:45
Sample: 1960 2023
Included observations: 64
Null Hypothesis: LINCOME has a unit root with a structural
          break in both the intercept and trend
Chosen lag length: 1 (maximum lags: 4)
Chosen break point: 1991

==============================================================================
                                                       t-Statistic     Prob. *
==============================================================================
Zivot-Andrews test statistic                             -6.461146    0.000000
1% critical value:                                           -5.57
5% critical value:                                           -5.08
10% critical value:                                          -4.82
==============================================================================
* Probability values are calculated from a standard t-distribution
  and do not take into account the breakpoint selection process
==============================================================================

    Read it carefully:

    • Chosen break point: 1991. The data found the reform year on their own. Nobody told the test.
    • The statistic is −6.46, beyond the 1% critical value of −5.57. We reject the unit root. Swarnapur's income is trend-stationary with a break.
    • Now look at the "Prob." column: 0.000000. The footnote is honest about it: this probability comes from an ordinary t-distribution and ignores the search over break dates. Treat it like a horoscope: entertaining, not evidence. Use the critical values.

    Other members of the break family

    Test What it adds
    Perron (1989) One break at a date you choose in advance
    Zivot-Andrews (1992) One break, date chosen by the data
    Perron-Vogelsang, Perron (1997) Breaks that happen gradually (the "innovational outlier" model)
    Lumsdaine-Papell (1997) Two breaks
    Lee-Strazicich (2003) One or two breaks, and breaks are allowed under the null too, so a break in a true random walk does not trigger a false rejection

    The lesson

    A unit root test sees one straight line. If history put a step in the road, the test will mistake the step for wandering. Before you declare a series a random walk, look at its picture and ask whether something big happened in the middle: a crisis, a reform, a pandemic, a change in how the data are measured.

    Something to think about

    The Zivot-Andrews test searches for the break that makes the series look most stationary. Suppose the true series is a random walk that happened to fall sharply in 2008. What might the test conclude, and which test in the table above was designed to avoid that mistake? Leave your thoughts in the comments.
