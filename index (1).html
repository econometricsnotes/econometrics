    The Short-Sighted Umpire: Power, DF-GLS and the Ng-Perron Tests

    Imagine a cricket umpire who is very careful never to give a batsman out wrongly. A noble goal. Unfortunately he achieves it by almost never raising his finger at all. Plumb LBW? "Not out." Edge to the keeper? "Not out, there may have been doubt." He never makes the first kind of mistake. He makes the second kind all day long.

    The Dickey-Fuller family of tests is that umpire. In this chapter we measure how short-sighted it is, and meet the tests built to give it glasses.

    Size and power, in plain words

    Every test can make two mistakes:

    Truth: unit root (balloon) Truth: stationary (kite)
    Test says "stationary" Type I error. Its chance is the size, set at 5%. Correct. Its chance is the power.
    Test says "unit root" Correct. Type II error. The short-sighted umpire.

    We choose the size ourselves: 5%. The power is whatever the test can manage, and for unit root tests it is often embarrassingly low.

    Measuring the umpire's eyesight

    I simulated 4,000 stationary series of length 100 for each value of \(\rho\) from 0.80 to 1.00, ran two tests on each, and counted how often each test correctly said "stationary":

    Read the red line. With \(\rho = 0.92\), the series is genuinely stationary; a shock has a half-life of about 8 periods. Yet the ordinary Dickey-Fuller test spots this only 23% of the time. More than three times out of four it says "unit root" about a series that does not have one. At \(\rho = 0.96\), its power is barely 11%.

    Why so bad? Over 100 observations, a kite on a long string and a balloon simply look alike. The string only starts to pull once the kite has drifted far enough, and with \(\rho\) near 1 that takes a long time. The test needs to tell 0.92 from 1.00 using a sample in which the difference has barely had a chance to show.

    The blue line is DF-GLS. At \(\rho = 0.92\) it catches the stationary series 47% of the time, about twice as often, while keeping the size near 5% when \(\rho = 1\). Where does the extra power come from?

    DF-GLS: clean the series first, then test

    Graham Elliott, Thomas Rothenberg and James Stock published the idea in Econometrica in 1996. You may know James Stock from the other side of your bookshelf: he is the Stock of Stock and Watson's Introduction to Econometrics.

    Their insight is about the constant (and trend). In the ordinary ADF regression, the constant is estimated by OLS alongside everything else. Under a near-unit root, OLS does a poor job of estimating the mean of a series that wanders slowly, and that poor estimate eats into the power of the test.

    ERS remove the mean in a smarter way, by GLS detrending. They pick a point "close to" a unit root, a local alternative

    \[ \bar\alpha = 1 + \frac{\bar c}{T}, \qquad \bar c = -7 \text{ (constant only)}, \quad \bar c = -13.5 \text{ (constant and trend)} \]

    and quasi-difference both the series and the deterministic terms:

    \[ \tilde y_1 = y_1, \qquad \tilde y_t = y_t - \bar\alpha\, y_{t-1}\ \ (t \ge 2) \]

    They then estimate the mean (and trend) by regressing \(\tilde y_t\) on the quasi-differenced constant (and trend), subtract the fitted deterministic part from \(y_t\), and call the result \(y_t^d\). Finally they run an ADF regression on \(y_t^d\) without any constant:

    \[ \Delta y_t^d = \delta\, y_{t-1}^d + \sum_{j=1}^p \gamma_j\, \Delta y_{t-j}^d + \varepsilon_t \]

    For \(T = 100\), \(\bar\alpha = 1 - 7/100 = 0.93\). The intuition: estimate the mean as if the series were nearly a random walk, which is exactly the situation in which ordinary OLS struggles.

    Where does \(\bar c = -7\) come from?

    ERS asked: if we knew the true alternative, what would the most powerful test be? Then they found the single alternative, \(\bar c = -7\) for the constant case, at which a test tuned to it has power of 50% against that alternative. A test tuned to this point turns out to be nearly as good as the best possible test at every alternative. It is like a pair of glasses ground for one distance that happens to work well at most distances.

    With only a constant, the DF-GLS statistic follows the Dickey-Fuller distribution for the "no constant" case, so its 5% critical value is about −1.94. With a trend, ERS supply their own table.

    ERS also proposed a point-optimal statistic, \(P_T = \left[S(\bar\alpha) - \bar\alpha\, S(1)\right] / \hat f_0\), which compares the sum of squared residuals of the GLS regression under \(\bar\alpha\) and under 1. EViews offers it as "Elliott-Rothenberg-Stock Point-Optimal". Small values reject the unit root.

    Ng-Perron: four statistics and a better lag rule

    Serena Ng and Pierre Perron (2001, Econometrica) took the GLS-detrended series and built four "modified" statistics on it. They also proposed the MAIC lag rule mentioned in Chapter 3. With \(\kappa = T^{-2}\sum_{t=2}^T (y_{t-1}^d)^2\) and \(\hat f_0\) an autoregressive estimate of the long-run variance:

    \[ MZ_\alpha = \frac{T^{-1}(y_T^d)^2 - \hat f_0}{2\kappa}, \qquad MSB = \left(\frac{\kappa}{\hat f_0}\right)^{1/2}, \qquad MZ_t = MZ_\alpha \times MSB \]

    plus \(MP_T\), a modified version of the ERS point-optimal statistic. You do not need to remember them. Just know three things:

    • They are designed to keep the size honest even when the shocks have the nasty negative moving-average structure that fools Phillips-Perron (Chapter 4).
    • All four reject the unit root for small values. For \(MZ_\alpha\) and \(MZ_t\), small means very negative. For \(MSB\) and \(MP_T\), which are always positive, small means close to zero.
    • Pierre Perron has now appeared in three chapters of this series. He is the Phillips of breakpoints.

    The data: solving the mystery

    Remember MYSTERY from Chapter 5? ADF could not reject a unit root, and KPSS could not reject stationarity. Stalemate.

    Is there a catch?

    There is always a catch.

    • More power near the unit root sometimes comes with slightly worse size in very small samples, especially with a trend.
    • These tests still assume no structural break. A break can make a kite look like a balloon to every test in this chapter. That's Chapter 7.
    • No test can create information that isn't in the data. With 30 annual observations and \(\rho = 0.95\), even DF-GLS will mostly say "unit root". Sometimes the honest answer is "I can't tell".

    The lesson

    "I could not reject a unit root" often means "my test was short-sighted", not "the series is a random walk". When the stakes are high, use a test with better eyesight. DF-GLS and Ng-Perron are available in the same EViews dialog as ADF, and they cost nothing to run.

    Something to think about

    Many studies in the 1980s concluded that real exchange rates have unit roots, which would mean purchasing power parity never holds, even in the long run. With what you now know about power, what would you say to the authors of those studies? What kind of data would you want to settle the question? (Hint: Chapter 8.)
